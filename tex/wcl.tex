\begin{abstract}
Downlink OFDMA scheduling in the IEEE 802.11ax WLANs requires a fast decision over discrete,
standard-compliant resource-unit (RU) layouts while accounting for the traffic
actually queued at the access point (AP). We formulate an exact instantaneous
service-rate oracle over legal 20~MHz RU partitions and imitate its decisions
from live AP queue states in ns-3.48. A compact permutation-equivariant set
network predicts per-station RU preferences, while a deterministic
combinatorial decoder guarantees a legal physical allocation. Across six
controlled scenarios, the learned throughput policy stays within 0.02~Mbps of
its oracle and raises saturated throughput from 63.99 to 92.12~Mbps relative
to the default round-robin scheduler. On a general-purpose Xeon host, neural
inference averages 175.3~$\mu$s and the complete scheduling hook 824.5~$\mu$s,
showing that optimization-quality decisions can be reproduced at microsecond
scale without running the oracle online. The present evaluation focuses on a
controlled single-BSS 20~MHz setting.
\end{abstract}
\begin{IEEEkeywords}
IEEE 802.11ax, OFDMA, resource allocation, imitation learning, ns-3.
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{D}{ownlink} orthogonal frequency-division multiple access (OFDMA)
lets an IEEE 802.11ax access point (AP) serve several stations (STAs) in one
transmission opportunity by assigning them different resource units (RUs).
This additional frequency-domain freedom can improve throughput and latency,
but only when RU sizes, selected users, queue contents, PHY rates, aggregation,
and common PPDU duration are coordinated carefully. Analyses and simulations
have shown that the gain of multi-user operation depends strongly on MAC/PHY
overhead and traffic conditions \cite{bellalta2019ap,daldoul2020performance,
avallone2021ofdma,magrin2023performance}. The resulting scheduler is therefore
not a simple bandwidth split: large RUs can drain short queues rapidly, whereas
small RUs increase concurrency, and the standard permits only specific physical
RU partition patterns.

A substantial body of work consequently formulates 802.11ax RU allocation as
an online optimization problem. Throughput-oriented algorithms jointly choose
users and legal RU layouts \cite{wang2020efficient,mmru2020}; QoS-aware
schedulers incorporate buffered traffic, priority, or fairness
\cite{filoso2020qos,brahmi2023fair}; and channel-aware methods additionally
exploit frequency-selective conditions and imperfect channel information
\cite{tutelian2021frequency}. More recent work uses buffer-aware OFDMA to
provide proportional resources to network slices \cite{marrone2025slicing}. Related work on mixed legacy/HE WLANs has further shown that the benefit of OFDMA depends on the interaction between OFDMA opportunity, AP contention, and fairness constraints, motivating population-aware parameter optimization \cite{GIANG2026112793}
These approaches demonstrate the value of state-aware scheduling, but solving
a combinatorial optimization problem at every opportunity can become costly
when the action must also respect the exact RU structures exposed by the MAC.

Learning-based scheduling offers another route. Buffer-state features including
packet sizes and ages have been used for deep reinforcement learning in
downlink OFDMA \cite{bansbach2021buffer}; PPO has been applied to
latency-constrained downlink Wi-Fi 6 scheduling \cite{he2023timesensitive}; and
multi-agent or hierarchical reinforcement learning has been proposed for
802.11ax uplink resource allocation \cite{han2024multiagent,noh2024joint}.
Practical systems further show that dynamic OFDMA scheduling can run on real
platforms: LOMAS implements latency-driven downlink scheduling on commercial
Wi-Fi 6 hardware \cite{tai2025lomas}, while interference-aware scheduling has
been demonstrated in real time on an openwifi software-defined-radio platform
\cite{havinga2026cti}. These results establish the practicality of adaptive and
learned scheduling, but leave a narrower question open: can a compact learned
policy reproduce the utility of an exact legal-action optimizer from live AP
queues, while preserving a direct contract between the optimization action,
the learning label, and the RU allocation actually executed by the MAC?

We answer this question through supervised oracle imitation. During data
collection, an exact finite-action optimizer labels each eligible live AP queue
state. At run time, a permutation-equivariant set network, following the set
processing principle of \cite{zaheer2017deep}, emits per-STA RU scores and a
deterministic combinatorial decoder projects them directly onto the same legal
physical action space used by the oracle. The actual frame exchange, sequence
assignment, aggregation, contention, acknowledgments, and retries remain inside
ns-3 rather than being replaced by a learned service model. This design makes
legality explicit by construction and moves the expensive PHY-aware oracle
search offline.

Across six controlled scenarios, the throughput-trained learned policy tracks
its oracle to within 0.02~Mbps and reaches 92.12~Mbps under saturation versus
63.99~Mbps for default round-robin. We also measure the complete host decision
path rather than neural inference alone: inference averages 175.3~$\mu$s and
the full scheduling hook 824.5~$\mu$s. An age-weighted variant is retained to
show objective-dependent generalization failures. The evaluation is intentionally
focused on a single 20~MHz BSS and does not claim long-run optimality across
arbitrary channels or traffic.

\section{Native Queue-State Oracle}
\label{sec:oracle}
\subsection{State and Legal Actions}
Consider at most $N=9$ STAs, one spatial stream, and one best-effort traffic
identifier (TID). At a downlink opportunity, the AP observes occupancy,
modulation and coding scheme (MCS) $m_i$, and the first $K_i\leq64$
BlockAck-eligible queued MPDUs for STA $i$. Packet $p$ has MAC payload size
$\ell_{ip}$, encoded MPDU size $b_{ip}$, and MAC enqueue age $d_{ip}$.
The eligibility check uses ns-3's queue and BlockAck machinery rather than
assuming every queued packet can be aggregated immediately.

Let $\cP$ contain legal 20~MHz HE RU partitions and $\cR(P)$ their physical
RU slots. Each RU has 26, 52, 106, or 242 tones. A binary assignment $z_{ir}$
maps STA $i$ to slot $r$ subject to
\begin{equation}
 \sum_{r\in\cR(P)}z_{ir}\leq1,\quad \sum_i z_{ir}\leq1,\quad
 1\leq\sum_{i,r}z_{ir}\leq M\leq9.
 \label{eq:legal}
\end{equation}
Empty or BlockAck-ineligible stations cannot be selected. Unused RUs are
allowed. An action $a=(P,z)$ therefore specifies both the partition and
an injective assignment to physical slots. Under the present channel model,
the utility of an RU depends on its size but not its frequency position.
Partitions with identical RU-size multisets are consequently represented by
one canonical physical layout. This equivalence would not hold with
frequency-selective channel quality or interference.

\subsection{Instantaneous Service Objective}
For a given partition and HE-SIG-B MCS class $c$, the implementation computes
the header duration $h(P,c)$ using ns-3's PHY timing functions. Here
$c=\min(5,\min_{i\text{ selected}}m_i)$. The header estimate accounts for all
slots in the chosen layout. For each eligible pair $(i,r)$, let $k_{ir}(P,c)$
be the longest queued prefix satisfying the negotiated A-MPDU byte limit,
the BlockAck window, and the 5.484~ms HE-MU duration limit. Delimiters and
padding enter the encoded aggregate size. ns-3's payload-duration function
returns its transmission time $t_{ir}(P,c)$.

The main label maximizes instantaneous payload service per estimated exchange
time. To examine objective dependence, define
\begin{align}
 W_{ir}(\lambda)&=\sum_{p=1}^{k_{ir}}\ell_{ip}
             \left(1+\lambda\frac{d_{ip}}{d_0}\right),\label{eq:weight}\\
 J_\lambda(a;s)&=
 \frac{8\sum_{i,r}z_{ir}W_{ir}(\lambda)}
 {T_{\mathrm{oh}}+h(P,c)+\max_{i,r:z_{ir}=1}t_{ir}},\label{eq:objective}\\
 a^*_\lambda(s)&\in\arg\max_{a\text{ satisfying }\eqref{eq:legal}}J_\lambda(a;s).
 \label{eq:optimal}
\end{align}
We use $T_{\mathrm{oh}}=100~\mu$s, a fixed approximation to contention/control
overhead, and $d_0=100$~ms. The throughput oracle O-T uses $\lambda=0$;
the age-weighted oracle O-A uses $\lambda=2$. Neither score includes a
packet-error probability. Positive $\lambda$ increases the reward for
serving older packets; it is a delay-pressure surrogate, not an objective
for future mean packet delay. Equations~\eqref{eq:weight}--\eqref{eq:optimal}
optimize RU allocation under a fixed longest-prefix aggregation rule;
they do not jointly optimize all shorter aggregation depths. Final MAC
admission can further restrict the predicted service.

\subsection{Exact Finite-Action Search}
For every $(P,c)$, enumerate the distinct feasible pair durations
$\tau\in\{t_{ir}(P,c)\}$. Retain pairs with $t_{ir}\leq\tau$ and
$\min(5,m_i)\geq c$. A dynamic program (DP) processes the physical RU slots.
Let $D_j(S)$ be the largest sum of rewards after $j$ slots with exactly
the STA subset $S$ assigned. Starting from $D_0(\varnothing)=0$ and
$D_0(S)=-\infty$ otherwise, its recurrence is
\begin{equation}
 D_{j+1}(S)=\max\left\{D_j(S),\ max_{i\in S}
      \big[D_j(S\setminus\{i\})+W_{i r_{j+1}}\big]\right\},
 \label{eq:dp}
\end{equation}
where the inner maximum admits only retained pairs and $|S|\leq M$.
The first term leaves an RU unused. Terminal sets must be nonempty and
include a STA realizing class $c$. Predecessors recover the assignment;
we evaluate its actual maximum pair duration in~\eqref{eq:objective} and
retain the best action over all thresholds and layouts.

\emph{Exactness under the stated model:} an optimal action has a maximum
pair duration equal to an enumerated threshold $\tau^*$. At its partition,
class, and terminal STA set, the DP finds a numerator at least as large
while the maximum pair duration is no greater than $\tau^*$. Its objective
is therefore at least that of the optimum. Since every reconstructed action
is feasible, equality follows. This establishes exactness of the finite
surrogate, not an upper bound on cumulative delivered traffic.

For $P_0$ canonical layouts, $C\leq6$ header classes, $L$ thresholds, and
$R\leq9$ slots, the DP core costs $O(P_0CLR N2^N)$ operations. The current
implementation scans up to $K=64$ prefix options inside pair lookups,
adding a factor $K$ to a conservative implementation bound. Predecessors
require $O(R2^N)$ storage per threshold. Enumerating this search for data
collection avoids requiring it at each learned-policy decision.

\section{Learning and Native Execution}
\subsection{Features and Training}
Each STA has 39 normalized inputs: eligibility, MCS, 20~MHz PHY rate, queue
packet and byte occupancy, head-of-line and mean observed-prefix age, and
size/age pairs for the first 16 eligible packets. Absent positions are zero.
Packet counts use $q/(q+64)$; byte occupancy is clipped at $64\times1600$;
packet size and age are clipped at 1600 bytes and 2000~ms. The learned
execution path reads at most 16 packets per STA. Oracle snapshots preserve
up to 64 packets; their mean-prefix-age feature therefore uses a potentially
longer prefix. This remaining feature mismatch is retained in the released
corpus rather than retrospectively altering the experiment.

A shared encoder maps row $x_i$ to $u_i$. Masked mean and maximum pooling
over eligible STAs produce global context
\begin{equation}
 g=\rho\left(\left[\operatorname{mean}_i u_i,
                           \operatorname{max}_i u_i\right]\right),\qquad
 v_i=\psi([u_i,g]).
\end{equation}
A linear head gives logits $v_{i,q}$ for
$q\in\{0,26,52,106,242\}$, where zero denotes no service. Encoder and
decoder each contain two width-64 ReLU layers; the context encoder has one.
Shared weights and symmetric pooling ensure that permuting input rows
permutes the logits. Decoder ties can still select a different equivalent
physical action after renumbering.

O-T and O-A generate separate corpora of \TRows{} and \ARows{} labeled
states from 24 trajectories each. The deterministic scenario generator
combines 3, 6, and 9 STAs; MCS 0, 3, 6, 9, and 11; four datagram sizes
(300, 800, 1200, and 1500 bytes); five load levels; and three skews.
Each collection run starts traffic at 1~s, uses 1~s warm-up and 0.3~s
measurement, and stops 0.1~s later. Labels are written throughout eligible
oracle operation, including warm-up. Empty actions do not create samples.

Four complete trajectories form validation, leaving 20 for training.
The throughput split contains \TTrainRows{} training and \TValidationRows{}
validation states. We train for 40 epochs with AdamW (learning rate
$10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$), batch size 256, dropout 0.05, gradient
clipping at 5, and random STA permutations. Class weights are proportional
to the inverse square root of training frequency. Cross entropy uses only
eligible rows; checkpoint selection maximizes validation exact-action
accuracy, then selection F1. No evaluation trajectory enters this split.
Only the RU head supplies scheduling decisions; unused legacy heads remain
in the checkpoint format.

\subsection{Legal Decoding and MAC Integration}
Independent argmax classes need not form a legal partition. The decoder solves
\begin{equation}
 \widehat a=\arg\max_{(P,z)\text{ satisfying }\eqref{eq:legal}}
   \sum_{i,r}z_{ir}\big(v_{i,\operatorname{type}(r)}-v_{i,0}\big).
 \label{eq:decode}
\end{equation}
The none-class subtraction accounts for leaving a STA unserved. Recurrence
\eqref{eq:dp}, now using logit differences, solves~\eqref{eq:decode} in
$O(P_0RN2^N)$ time without PHY evaluation or threshold enumeration. Any
nonempty decoded action obeys the structural RU constraints by construction.
This property guarantees legal placement, not successful MAC admission or
reception. Empty actions use the inherited scheduling path.

The exported binary occupies \ModelBytes{} bytes and uses a C++ runtime
without a tensor-library dependency. We extend
\texttt{RrMultiUserScheduler}, install the partition code and each physical
\texttt{HeRu::RuSpec} in the \texttt{WifiTxVector}, and extend mixed-layout
HE-SIG-B handling. ns-3 then performs sequence assignment, actual aggregation,
contention, acknowledgments, and retries. Unit checks cover simple oracle
decisions, age weighting, and legal projection; the artifact includes the
upstream RU-allocation, OFDMA, aggregation, queue, and duration test commands.
No result here relies on substituting predicted service for received traffic.

\section{Native ns-3 Evaluation}
\subsection{Protocol and Baselines}
\input{scenarios.tex}
The network has a single AP, stationary STAs on a 2~m circle, 5~GHz
20~MHz HE operation, 800~ns guard interval, one spatial stream, and a common
fixed MCS per run. We use \texttt{SpectrumWifiPhy}, log-distance path loss,
UDP periodic arrivals, no A-MSDU, a 64-MPDU BlockAck window, and no uplink
OFDMA. Other settings retain the packaged ns-3 defaults. For STA index $i$
starting at zero, offered rate is $b[1+\eta((i\bmod3)-1)]$ Mbps.
Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} gives the six evaluation cases. The 1,000-byte
datagram size is absent from training. These held-out configurations test
interpolation in a controlled family, not unrestricted traffic generalization.

RR4 is ns-3's credit round-robin scheduler with its default four-STA limit.
Max-Q sorts eligible destinations by backlog and retains the stock RU
allocation path, with a nine-STA limit. O-T/O-A and their learned policies
L-T/L-A allow nine STAs and legal mixed partitions. Thus RR4 is an operational
default baseline; the study does not isolate learning from the effect of
different station limits and allocation capabilities. Max-Q controls the
station limit, but not the mixed-layout action space.

Each of six policies is run on each scenario with ten paired RNG run numbers
(920000--920009), giving 360 runs. Traffic starts at 1~s; measurement covers
$[3,4]$~s after 2~s of traffic warm-up. The simulation ends at 4.1~s.
Throughput counts UDP bytes received in the measurement window divided by
one second, including the sequence/timestamp header. Delay is measured from
UDP generation for those received packets. It excludes dropped or still
queued packets and is conditioned on reception time. Paired confidence
intervals use Student's $t$ with nine degrees of freedom; the six-scenario
analysis is exploratory without multiplicity correction.

\subsection{Throughput and Objective Dependence}
\input{throughput.tex}
\begin{figure}[t]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{gains.pdf}
 \caption{L-T gains in the four overloaded cases; bars are paired 95\%
 confidence intervals over ten runs. Baseline capabilities differ as stated
 in Section~IV-A.}
 \label{fig:gains}
\end{figure}
Table~\ref{tab:throughput} reports all six policies. L-T obtains 92.12~Mbps
versus RR4's 63.99~Mbps at saturation, a 44.0\% gain, and exceeds Max-Q
by 26.22~Mbps. The paired gains over RR4 are $28.13\pm0.82$~Mbps at
saturation, $5.69\pm0.22$~Mbps at low MCS, $19.28\pm0.03$~Mbps with
four STAs, and $18.57\pm0.78$~Mbps with seven STAs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:gains}). These effects are repeatable for the tested
configuration. The four-STA result cannot be attributed to a higher
recipient cap, since both policies can address all four STAs; it still
combines allocation and policy differences.

L-T is within 0.02~Mbps of O-T in every case. This is a comparison of
closed-loop throughput means, not a bound on instantaneous objective regret.
Raw exact-action accuracy is only \TExact\%, and accuracy on eligible STA
rows is \TActiveAccuracy\%. Such discrepancies can arise from equivalent
actions and the constraint decoder; their individual contributions have not
been isolated. A direct greedy decoder and objective-regret evaluation are
needed before attributing the gain solely to learning.

At sparse and normal load the policies are offer-limited. Normal RR4's
49.83~Mbps slightly exceeds the 49.50~Mbps offered rate because the
measurement can include packets generated during warm-up. The same boundary
effect permits small deviations above offered rate in other cases.
Short measurement windows therefore require particular caution near
the transition to saturation.

L-A does not uniformly reproduce O-A: it loses 9.74~Mbps with four STAs
and 8.54~Mbps with seven STAs, despite higher raw exact-action accuracy
(\AExact\%). At saturation it obtains 83.75~Mbps and Jain's index 0.926,
compared with L-T's 92.12~Mbps and 0.907. These indices describe equality
of received per-STA throughput; offered rates are unequal. The age weight
changes the operating point but does not establish an optimal fairness or
delay tradeoff. Both training objectives and all underlying runs are included
in the artifact.

\subsection{Interpreting Packet Delay}
\input{delay.tex}
Table~\ref{tab:delay} shows lower received-packet mean delay for L-T in the
four overloaded cases, but these are transient measurements. At saturation,
offered load is 144~Mbps and L-T serves about 64\% of that rate. Neither
policy can sustain all arrivals under this configuration. With four STAs,
L-T nearly serves the entire 88.8~Mbps offered load while RR4 serves about
78\%; the large delay difference primarily accompanies this service-capacity
gap. The ratio of received throughput to offered rate is not a measured
packet-cohort delivery probability.

Finite buffers, queue expiry, and the observation boundary further affect
which packets contribute to delay. We therefore claim neither queue
stability nor a steady-state delay improvement under overload. At sparse
load L-T's mean delay rises from RR4's 0.56 to 0.87~ms. Stable-load sweeps
with explicit drop, backlog, and packet-cohort accounting are required for
a general latency claim. In particular, adding age to~\eqref{eq:weight}
has not solved average-delay optimization.

\subsection{Computation Cost}
\input{timing.tex}
\begin{figure}[t]
 \centering
 \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{latency.pdf}
 \caption{Serial L-T host cost by scenario. Stacks show mean component
 times; black ticks show means of run-level decision p99s. The dashed line
 is the chosen 1.5~ms budget. Minor hook bookkeeping is outside the stacks.}
 \label{fig:latency}
\end{figure}
An independent serial campaign uses three RNG runs per scenario
(940000--940002). The host is an Intel Xeon Gold 5222 at nominal 3.80~GHz;
GCC 14.2.0 builds the packaged ns-3 default profile with
\texttt{-Os -g -DNDEBUG} and ns-3 assertions/logging enabled.
\texttt{std::chrono::steady\_clock} measures state extraction, model
inference, projection, and the complete selection hook. The hook includes
assignment bookkeeping and station ordering, but subsequent MAC frame
construction and hardware submission are outside the measured interval.
Timers cover all instrumented opportunities, including warm-up and drain.

The overall mean inference and hook costs are \InferenceMean{} and
\DecisionMean{}~$\mu$s, respectively (Table~\ref{tab:timing}). Projection
dominates at nine STAs (Fig.~\ref{fig:latency}); a small network alone
does not ensure a small complete scheduling cost. The reported
\DecisionPninetyNine{}~$\mu$s is the average of 18 within-run p99s,
not a pooled p99. The largest observed hook time is
\DecisionMaximum{}~$\mu$s. At an illustrative 1.5~ms budget, the mean
run miss fraction is \MissPercent\%; this is neither a deadline derived
from the MAC protocol nor a worst-case guarantee. Empty-action fallback
averages \FallbackMean{} occurrences per run, including initial setup;
fallbacks are distinct from deadline misses.

The timing results are already encouraging for real-time use. The neural
forward pass averages only \InferenceMean{}~$\mu$s on a general-purpose Xeon,
so model inference itself is firmly in the microsecond regime; even the complete
selection hook averages \DecisionMean{}~$\mu$s. Modern AP processors with SIMD
support or dedicated neural accelerators, analogous to Jetson-class embedded
AI hardware, can plausibly reduce the forward-pass cost further. In the current
implementation, however, projection costs 483.0~$\mu$s on average and therefore
dominates inference at nine STAs. Optimizing this legal decoder, caching common
partition decisions, or implementing it with more parallel hardware is thus at
least as important as accelerating the neural network. Together, these numbers
indicate substantial headroom for practical real-time integration rather than
a computation bottleneck intrinsic to oracle imitation.

Computation time is nevertheless not charged to ns-3 simulated time, and the
1.5~ms line is an illustrative engineering budget rather than a deadline derived
from the 802.11ax MAC. Consequently, the present measurements establish
microsecond-scale host software cost and make real-time deployment plausible,
but do not by themselves constitute a device-level deadline guarantee. A final
deployment study should inject computation delay and stale state into the loop
and measure the implementation on the target AP SoC. The exponential STA-mask
decoder also limits direct extrapolation beyond nine STAs.

\section{Conclusion}
An oracle and learned decoder sharing legal physical RU actions provide a
reproducible route from live AP queues to native downlink OFDMA scheduling.
In six controlled ns-3 cases, the throughput policy closely tracks its
oracle and improves overloaded throughput over default RR. The age-weighted
model's failures, unequal baseline capabilities, and conditional delay
statistics constrain the interpretation. Host timing shows that the learned network already operates at
microsecond scale and identifies legal projection, rather than inference, as
the main remaining compute cost. This makes deployment on stronger AP CPUs or
embedded ML accelerators technically plausible. Longer runs, matched mixed-RU
heuristics, heterogeneous time-varying channels, and interference experiments
remain necessary to assess robustness. The accompanying source artifact provides
the simulator snapshot, data, models, and commands for regenerating every
reported table and figure.
